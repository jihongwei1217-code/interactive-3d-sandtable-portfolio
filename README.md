# Interactive 3D Sandtable Platform

> A sanitized product case study covering an AI-assisted, browser-based 3D planning and delivery workflow for a robotics pilot-test environment.

## Overview

This project explored how a physical robotics test environment could be translated into an interactive digital sandtable and a print-oriented delivery workflow.

Instead of treating 3D models as isolated files, the product organized the complete user journey:

1. Browse equipment and scene modules.
2. Preview assets in the browser.
3. Arrange a pilot-test environment interactively.
4. Save and compare layout ideas.
5. Prepare modular assets for physical prototyping and 3D printing.
6. Navigate delivery documents and implementation materials.

The public repository intentionally contains only a sanitized case study. Proprietary geometry, internal documents, company data, and production source files are excluded.

## My Role

I worked as the product owner and AI-assisted prototyping coordinator. My responsibilities included:

- translating photos, field observations, and stakeholder feedback into product requirements;
- defining the information architecture for the 3D asset catalog, scene planner, downloads, and delivery navigation;
- prioritizing interaction improvements such as drag-and-drop placement, camera recovery, undo, deletion, history, and modular scene composition;
- coordinating the relationship between browser previews, printable assets, assembly documentation, and deployment;
- conducting repeated visual and usability reviews and documenting actionable feedback;
- using AI tools to accelerate prototyping, interface iteration, documentation, and delivery;
- coordinating deployment and preparing a shareable project experience.

My contribution focused on product ownership, requirements, interaction logic, AI-assisted prototyping, quality review, deployment coordination, and delivery design. 3D geometry and implementation were managed as cross-functional deliverables. The value of my work was turning a fragmented modelling task into a coherent, testable product and delivery system.

## Product Decisions

### 1. Build a workflow, not a model viewer

A model viewer alone would not solve the operational problem. The product therefore connected asset discovery, scene planning, print preparation, and delivery documentation.

### 2. Design for different users

The experience needed to serve several groups:

- managers reviewing the overall concept;
- project teams planning the scene;
- modelling and printing partners receiving production requirements;
- colleagues opening the project without specialist 3D software.

### 3. Treat physical feasibility as a product constraint

Visual quality was not enough. Components also needed reasonable scale, support, wall thickness, modularity, and assembly logic.

### 4. Use AI as an execution accelerator

AI-assisted development helped shorten the loop between feedback and a testable interface. Human judgment remained responsible for scope, requirements, quality control, and stakeholder alignment.

## Representative Iterations

- Reframed the product from a static showcase into an interactive planning tool.
- Connected indoor and outdoor scenes into a unified sandtable concept.
- Added modular equipment discovery and browser previews.
- Improved object deletion, undo, viewpoint recovery, and history concepts.
- Introduced print-oriented delivery and assembly navigation.
- Iterated on realism, scale, physical support, and scene density based on stakeholder feedback.

## What I Learned

- A visually impressive prototype can still fail if it does not match the real workflow.
- Product managers working with 3D systems must understand both digital interaction and physical constraints.
- AI can dramatically increase iteration speed, but weak requirements only produce faster rework.
- The best portfolio evidence is not the number of screens—it is a clear chain from problem to decision, working demo, feedback, and outcome.
